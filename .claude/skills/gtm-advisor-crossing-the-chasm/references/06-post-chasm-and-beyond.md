# 6. Leaving the Chasm Behind — and What Comes After

Sources: *Crossing the Chasm*, 3rd ed., Conclusion, Appendix 1 (High-Tech Market Development
Model, summarizing *Inside the Tornado*), Appendix 2 (The Four Gears Model).

## The fundamental lesson of the post-chasm period

**The post-chasm enterprise is bound by the commitments made by the pre-chasm enterprise.**

Commitments made in haste to get a foothold in the early market are frequently unmaintainable
afterward — they promise a level of performance or reward that would destroy the company if
delivered. So an early task of the post-chasm era is managing out of contradictions created
before the crossing: devaluing assets, demoting people whose titles outran their fit, and moving
authority over product and technology. Moore is candid that this can be a very nasty period,
ending in bitter disappointment and resentment.

The best solution is to avoid making the wrong commitments in the first place. When advising a
pre-chasm company, flag these as future liabilities *now*:

- Sales comp plans built on visionary-sized, project-shaped deals.
- Custom development promises to marquee early customers that mortgage the mainstream roadmap.
- Titles and equity granted for early-market performance that will not survive mainstream roles.
- Valuations set on early-market growth rates that the chasm will interrupt.
- Partner and channel agreements written for early-market economics.

Three arenas change alongside marketing: **finance, organizational development, and R&D.**

## Pioneers and settlers

The hardest organizational transition. Two kinds of pioneer:

**Technology pioneers** — the people who create the discontinuous innovation. In the mainstream
they must accept incremental improvement, compatibility constraints, quality processes, and
documentation, which are foreign and often repugnant to them.

**Sales pioneers** — the people who can sell a vision that does not exist yet. Left unchecked
after the crossing, a pioneer salesperson keeps signing visionary-shaped deals that rob the
mainstream R&D effort to fund custom work — putting enormous strain on engineering and, at the
extreme, taking the company backward.

The honest framing: **pioneers do not want to settle down**, and it is not in their interest or
the company's to pretend otherwise. Settlers do not take pioneers' places; they take *different*
places — ones pioneers never occupied and would never choose. Settlers make things repeatable,
predictable, and boring, which is exactly what pragmatists want and exactly what pioneers find
intolerable.

**Two transitional roles** bridge the gap. Both are temporary, both are stepping-stones to
conventional jobs afterward:

- **Target market segment manager** — owns the beachhead end to end: making the segment develop.
  Often staffed by converting the pioneer salesperson who owned the early-market account,
  chartered as the segment's account manager, and then handing the account to a settler once the
  segment is real.
- **Whole product manager** — owns getting the doughnut to 100%: the partner recruitment, the
  gap closure, the delivery quality that the marketing promise depends on. Moore names pulling
  the partner group together as the major challenge of this role.

## The bowling alley — what to do next

The beachhead is the first pin. Extend into adjacent segments that overlap with either:

- the target customer's **word-of-mouth community** (same buyer type, different industry), or
- the **partner ecosystem** delivering the whole product (same industry, different use case).

The tactics for each next niche are identical to chasm crossing, but time and effort diminish
because you leverage prior wins. In the bowling alley, the channel still favors value over
volume, pricing keeps a premium over commodity, and positioning still works as the intersection
of the incumbent market alternative's domain expertise and the next-generation product
alternative's performance.

Advisory move: always leave the client with two named next pins and the specific asset (reference,
partner, or whole-product component) that each one reuses.

## The full market development model

Five states, each rewarding a different approach:

**Early Market** — enthusiasts and visionaries; whole product incomplete; deals structured as
projects with "whatever it takes" commitments; partners are large SIs maintaining a customer
relationship; direct sales; price-insensitive; no competition, only resistance from the status quo.

**The Chasm** — see everything above.

**Bowling Alley** — beachhead plus adjacent niches; value-oriented channel; premium pricing;
positioning as the intersection of market and product alternatives.

**Tornado** — a state change, like water to steam. The target customer becomes generic as whole
sectors adopt at once; the compelling reason to buy is that the technology is now must-have
infrastructure. Whole product assembles at the point of sale; partnerships convert into pecking
orders; distribution goes to the lowest-cost, furthest-reaching channel that can meet service
levels. Reference pricing shifts from the market leader to the low-cost commodity provider —
products are priced *up* from the bottom instead of *down* from the top. Positioning becomes
price-performance plus market share. Winning #1 share, especially on proprietary technology with
high switching costs, creates enormous shareholder value.

**Main Street** — growth shifts from double/triple-digit to single-digit and becomes cyclical.
The commodity offer runs an operational-excellence strategy at the low-cost buyer; value-added
offers run customer intimacy at segments with discretionary income. Those extras fit into the
existing whole product without extra effort — "whole product + 1" — typically priced as a 10–15%
add-on at five to ten times the base margin, sometimes with the base subsidized to secure
consumption revenue. Self-serve is the ideal channel because overhead kills the profit.
Positioning rests on incumbency or on a secondary attribute; for core functionality, good enough
is good enough.

**Total Assimilation** — the category is infrastructure.

Moore's core lesson across state transitions: **make a coherent bet with everyone rowing in the
same direction and be wrong** — you can change course fast — rather than delay and waffle, which
teaches you nothing and returns suboptimally at every step. Getting the company aligned on which
state it is in is often the whole job.

## The Four Gears — when the chasm model does not apply

Crossing the chasm is unapologetically a **B2B model**. It applies wherever institutions must do
heavy lifting to adopt a disruptive technology. Consumer digital products (Moore's examples:
Google, Facebook, YouTube, Skype) reached scale without crossing a chasm; their path resembles
consumer packaged goods — trial, test markets, launch, mass promotion — with digital differences.

**Use the Four Gears instead when the client is a consumer/digital-adoption business.** Four
activities, not a linear sequence:

1. **Acquire traffic**
2. **Engage users**
3. **Monetize engagement**
4. **Enlist the faithful**

How they actually turn:

- **Engagement comes first.** Can you create an experience compelling and differentiated enough
  that users repeat it, many times? Repetition establishes a pattern of consumption — the first
  underpinning of a mass market.
- **Then acquisition**, interacting with engagement, each modifying the other. The question is
  whether the compelling experience *scales* — on the demand side (new users, eventually ones
  wanting something different from your first users) and the supply side (new content or
  features). Scaling always requires modifying the offer, and modifying the offer always affects
  scaling.
- **The tipping point.** Before it, every bit of scale requires pumped-in fuel and the system
  reverts if you stop. After it, the system restabilizes around a new status quo and pulls you
  forward. Where it lies is only visible in the rearview mirror. The goal of the acquire–engage
  phase is to get past it as fast as possible.
- **Enlistment** — hyper-engaging a small, vocal minority who evangelize because your product has
  become part of their identity. You do not pay them; paying them would be insulting. Moore maps
  it to NPS: 9–10 fuels viral growth and collapses acquisition cost; 7–8 does not go viral but
  prevents churn; 1–6 signals openness to switching or outright counter-evangelism. Target:
  churn below ~2%/month (roughly a four-year customer lifetime), and during category growth, use
  enlistment to accelerate past the tipping point.
- **Monetization last, and feathered in.** The consumer pattern is "URL" — *Ubiquity now, Revenue
  Later*. Monetization *will* slow the other three gears. Invoke it too early or too hard and you
  pop the clutch and stall the engine. The art is feathering it so the retarding effect is
  absorbed and the engine returns to full speed quickly, while running continuous pricing
  experiments.

Moore's forward view: the two models increasingly work as a pincer — grassroots consumer adoption
generating waves, institutional marketing capitalizing on them — with the B2C course in the
lead, because until there is proven traction on the four gears there is nothing for a
monetization engine to feed on. Most likely where public and private funding intersect:
health care, education, public services.

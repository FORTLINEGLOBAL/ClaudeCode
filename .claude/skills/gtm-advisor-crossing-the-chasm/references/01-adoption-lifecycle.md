# 1. The Technology Adoption Life Cycle and the Chasm

Source: Geoffrey A. Moore, *Crossing the Chasm*, 3rd ed. (2014), Introduction and chapters 1–2.
This file is a working synthesis for advisory use, not a substitute for the book.

## What a market is

Moore's definition is the load-bearing one in the whole framework. A market is:

- a set of actual or potential customers
- for a given set of products or services
- who have a common set of needs or wants
- **who reference each other when making a buying decision**

The fourth clause is the one people skip and the one that matters. If the people in your
"segment" do not talk to each other, it is not a market — it is a demographic. Word of mouth
inside a referencing community is the mechanism that makes niche domination work, and it is the
mechanism that makes a scattered, cross-industry customer list worthless as a base.

Test to apply to any proposed segment: *if we win five of these customers, will the sixth hear
about it without us telling them?* If no, reject the segment.

## The five psychographics

Adoption is driven by attitude toward discontinuous innovation, not by demographics.

**Technology enthusiasts (innovators)** — buy the technology for its own sake. They are the
gatekeepers: their blessing gives everyone else permission to look. They need very little whole
product; Moore's line is that real techies don't need whole products. They have little money and
no organizational authority. Value them as early evidence and as a source of honest technical
critique, never as a revenue base.

**Visionaries (early adopters)** — buy a dream, not a product. They are looking for an
order-of-magnitude improvement that gives them strategic advantage and personal career
advancement. They are price-insensitive (they want margin in the deal so you can service them
properly), they will fund custom work, and they will act as your first big references. They are
also dangerous: they demand customization that pulls R&D off the roadmap, they expect the vendor
to "do whatever it takes," and their endorsement carries no weight with pragmatists — it may
actively repel them.

**Pragmatists (early majority)** — roughly a third of the whole cycle, and the gatekeepers of the
mainstream. They buy to fix a broken process, not to reinvent one. Characteristics that drive
every tactic in this framework:
- They want the whole product, complete, from a vendor who will still exist in five years.
- They communicate **vertically** — inside their own industry — unlike enthusiasts and
  visionaries, who communicate horizontally. This is why niche domination compounds and why
  breaking into a new industry is so hard.
- Catch-22: they will not buy from you until you are established, and you cannot become
  established until they buy.
- They **want to see competition** — for price leverage, for a fallback, and to confirm you are
  the proven leader of a real category. An empty competitive set reads as risk, not innovation.
- They buy the market leader, because market leadership means third parties will build
  supporting products around you. Aftermarket, not features.
- They will pay a premium for the leader — Moore puts it as high as 30% over the competitive set.
- They prefer a minimum number of vendor relationships and like a single point of control (a
  reason VARs and systems integrators work well as a route in).
- Once won inside a vertical, they are loyal and will help you succeed.

**Conservatives (late majority)** — buy only when the technology is fully institutionalized and
cheap. Cost-based pricing. Not a chasm-crossing target, but a large and profitable late market.

**Skeptics (laggards)** — not a market. Their function is to point at the gap between what was
promised and what was delivered, which is genuinely useful intelligence about whole-product holes.

## The cracks and the chasm

There are cracks between every pair of adjacent groups, because each group's references and
buying logic are unintelligible to the next.

- Enthusiast → visionary: a crack when the technology has no dream attached to it, no
  order-of-magnitude business consequence.
- Pragmatist → conservative: a crack when the product is still too hard to use or requires
  in-house expertise.
- **Visionary → pragmatist: the chasm.** Not a crack. A gap.

The chasm exists because the visionary is the *worst possible reference* for the pragmatist.
Pragmatists want references from other pragmatists in their own industry with the same broken
process. Visionaries are, by construction, the people in the industry least like the pragmatist —
they are trying to break the process, not fix it. So at the moment the early market saturates,
the company has revenue, momentum, a customer list, and precisely zero of the currency the next
buyer accepts.

Secondary causes that show up in every chasm case:
- The early-market business is **project-shaped** — custom deliverables, "whatever it takes"
  commitments — which does not scale and quietly consumes the R&D that the mainstream product
  needs.
- The whole product is nowhere near complete, and visionaries did not care because they were
  willing to assemble it themselves.
- Sales, comp plans, and forecasts were built on visionary deal shapes and keep predicting deals
  that no longer close.

## The symptom checklist (use this to diagnose)

A company is in the chasm when several of these are true:

- Early deals were large, custom, sold on vision, and sponsored by a single ambitious executive.
- New prospects say "great presentation," ask for more information, and never issue a PO.
- Sales cycles have lengthened with no change in win rate.
- Every deal in the pipeline is a different use case in a different industry.
- References exist but the wrong people gave them.
- Engineering is split between mainstream roadmap and custom commitments to two or three
  marquee accounts.
- Revenue is flat or lumpy after a period of fast growth, and the team's explanation is
  "we need more leads" or "we need better sales people."

## What actually gets you across

The whole rest of the framework, in one line: pick one pragmatist segment small enough that you
can be the market leader in it, deliver 100% of the whole product for that one segment's single
compelling reason to buy, and let the segment's internal referencing do the selling. Then use
that segment as the first bowling pin.

Moore's core economic argument for niching: the total available market is the destination, but
you fund and justify the plan on the **bowling alley** — the beachhead plus the adjacent niches it
opens — not on the beachhead alone (too small to fund) and not on the aggregate mass market
(not credible, and it destroys focus).

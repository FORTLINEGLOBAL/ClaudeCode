# 5. Launch the Invasion — Distribution and Pricing

Source: *Crossing the Chasm*, 3rd ed., chapter 7.

Distribution and pricing are the only two marketing decisions that touch the mainstream customer
directly. Distribution is the vehicle; pricing is the fuel. With distribution in particular you
usually get one chance, which is why these come last, after everything else is nailed down.

## The number-one objective

**Secure a distribution channel into the mainstream market that the pragmatist customer is
comfortable buying from.** Moore is emphatic that this comes before revenue, before profit,
before press, even before customer satisfaction — everything else can be fixed later, but only
if the channel exists. If the channel is not established, nothing further can be accomplished.

And therefore: **during the chasm period, the number-one job of pricing is to motivate the
channel** — not to satisfy the customer and not to satisfy investors.

The formula: attract *customer-oriented distribution* using *distribution-oriented pricing* as
the primary lure.

## Customer-oriented distribution — five buyer types

Pick the channel from who the buyer is, not from what the company prefers or what it already has.

**1. Enterprise executives** making big-ticket decisions on complex systems adopted broadly.
Expect hundreds of thousands to millions, and a consultative sales experience that identifies
their needs and custom-fits the offer.
→ **Direct sales**, top-down. The marketing is *relationship marketing*: thought-leadership
forums for a handful of senior executives, followed by personal contact and referral down into
the organization. Expensive, slow, and the only thing that works at this deal size.

**2. End users** making low-cost decisions on personal or workgroup technology adopted locally.
→ **Web self-service / frictionless purchase.** The product must sell and onboard itself.

**3. Department heads** making medium-cost decisions on use-case-specific solutions adopted
inside their own org.
→ **Inside sales / "Sales 2.0"** — a blended model: demand generated online, qualified and
closed by a lower-cost inside team, often with a free trial or pilot as the qualifying step.
This is the most common landing zone for B2B chasm crossings today.

**4. Engineers** making design decisions for products their company sells on.
→ **Two-tier distribution** with technically fluent reps and distributors; design wins, reference
designs, long qualification cycles.

**5. Small business owner-operators** making modest but highly material purchases with limited
capital and a strong need to see value back.
→ **VARs and local specialists**, or a self-serve product with an unusually complete onboarding
whole product.

Two standing notes:
- VARs and systems integrators specializing in the target vertical are especially attractive to
  pragmatists because they deliver a turnkey answer and a **single point of control** — one
  company to call when something goes wrong.
- You pick a **primary** channel and keep it for a long time. Add coverage later for other
  segments. Channel/strategy fit matters enormously, and there is no shame in switching if the
  first choice does not bear fruit.

## Pricing from three perspectives

### Customer-oriented pricing — varies by psychographic

- **Visionaries: value-based pricing.** Relatively price-insensitive. Seeking an
  order-of-magnitude return, they treat immediate cost as noise, and they actively want margin in
  the price so the vendor can fund the special service they will demand. There is even prestige
  in buying the expensive option.
- **Pragmatists: competition-based pricing.** They want the market leader and will pay a premium
  for it — Moore puts it as high as **30%** over the competitive set — because leadership lowers
  total cost of ownership and brings an aftermarket. But the allowed price is still a function of
  comparison with the other players. If you are not the leader, apply the rule in reverse and
  discount.
- **Conservatives: cost-based pricing.** They waited, and low price is their reward.

So during the crossing, price is set by the competitive set you created in chapter 6: a premium
over the **market alternative** because you have next-generation technology, and a premium over
the **product alternative** because you have invested in a segment-specific whole product.

### Vendor-oriented pricing — the weakest basis during the chasm

Cost of goods, cost of sales, overhead, cost of capital, target returns. Necessary for running
the business, but with no immediate meaning in the market. Its real market effects are indirect:
it sets the price-point ballpark, which sets whether you are in direct sales, web self-service,
or Sales 2.0; and it determines how many transactions you need, which determines whether you
manage the top, middle, or bottom of the funnel.

Moore's verdict: vendor-oriented pricing is the **least sound** basis for pricing decisions during
the chasm, because this is the period when you must be almost entirely externally focused.

### Distribution-oriented pricing — the one that governs

Two questions:

- **Is it priced to sell?** Price must not become a major issue in the sales cycle. Companies
  coming off early-market success with visionaries almost always price too high. When the channel
  reports price resistance and cites comparable products, the company's reflex is to insist it has
  no competition and the channel doesn't know how to sell. That reflex is the error.
- **Is it worthwhile to sell?** Price can also be too *low* to cross the chasm. If margin does
  not reward the channel for the extra effort of introducing a disruptive product into an
  established customer relationship, the channel will not do it. The reward has to be
  significantly better than business as usual.

**The rule:** set price at the market-leader price point — reinforcing (or at least not
undercutting) your leadership claim — and build a disproportionately high channel reward into the
margin, to be phased out as the product becomes established and competition for the right to
distribute it increases.

## The four launch principles

1. The prime goal is access to a **customer-oriented distribution channel** — the one mainstream
   pragmatists would expect and want to buy from.
2. The **channel type follows the price point**. If the long-term channel is not direct sales,
   you may need a supplementary or alternative demand-creation channel during the crossing to
   stimulate early mainstream acceptance.
3. **Price carries a message.** The only acceptable message is market leadership, so price
   relative to your identified competitive set.
4. **Margins are the channel's reward.** Crossing the chasm puts extra pressure on the channel
   and often leverages equity the channel has built with pragmatist customers. Pay a premium
   margin during the chasm period.

## Output for the client

- Named primary channel, with the buyer type that justifies it.
- Any supplementary demand-creation channel needed for the crossing, with its sunset condition.
- Price point, with the explicit comparison to market alternative and product alternative that
  sets it.
- Channel margin during the crossing, and the trigger that phases it back to normal.

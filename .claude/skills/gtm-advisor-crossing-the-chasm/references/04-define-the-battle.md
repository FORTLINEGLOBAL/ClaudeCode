# 4. Define the Battle — Competition and Positioning

Source: *Crossing the Chasm*, 3rd ed., chapter 6.

## The rule of engagement

Any force can defeat any other force — if it can define the battle. If you set the turf and the
winning criteria, you should not lose. When companies do lose, it is usually because they
misread what the target customer actually wants, or are unwilling to take responsibility for
delivering it.

## Creating the competition

This is, in Moore's words, the single most important marketing communication decision in the
battle to enter the mainstream.

The pragmatist buyer insists on seeing viable competition. Coming out of the early market, you
probably have none in any form a pragmatist recognizes — you developed a new value proposition
with visionaries. So you must **create** a competitive set: position inside a buying category
that already has credibility with pragmatists, populated with other reasonable choices they
already know, and then be the indisputably correct choice within it.

The failure mode is rigging the set. You can always build a comparison you dominate, but it will
be either not credible or not attractive. Moore's own example: he might claim to be the greatest
high-tech marketing consultant with a PhD in Renaissance English literature (credible, not
attractive), or the greatest marketing consultant of all time (attractive, not credible).

The fix is to build the set around **pragmatist** values, not visionary ones — which is what the
compass is for.

## The Competitive Positioning Compass

Four domains of value in high tech: **technology, product, market, company.** Which domain the
customer cares about changes as the product moves through the life cycle.

- Horizontal axis: buyer interest in and understanding of technology — **specialists** on one
  side, **generalists** on the other. The early market is dominated by specialists who care about
  technology and product. The mainstream is dominated by generalists who care about market
  leadership and company stability, not speeds and feeds.
- Vertical axis: buyer attitude toward the value proposition — **skepticism** to **support**.
  Markets begin skeptical and evolve toward support. Technology enthusiasts are the skeptical
  gatekeepers of the early market; pragmatists are the skeptical gatekeepers of the mainstream.
  Once the gatekeeper blesses it, the companion group (visionaries, then conservatives) is free
  to buy.

The four value domains map to the quadrants:

| | Skeptical | Supportive |
|---|---|---|
| **Specialist** | Technology | Product |
| **Generalist** | Market | Company |

**Crossing the chasm is the transition from product-based values to market-based values.**

Two operating consequences:

- People who support you take an interest in your product and your company. People who are
  skeptical of you do not. So at the beginning of a market, when skepticism is the default,
  basing your communications on product or company strengths is a mistake — you have no
  permission to tout them, because the market does not yet believe you will be around.
- Positioning is about the **audience's** state of mind, not yours. Most failed positioning
  statements come from vendors unable to see themselves from someone else's point of view.

## The two reference competitors

The single most useful mechanic in the chapter. You need exactly two, and they do different jobs.

**Market alternative** — a vendor the target customer has been buying from for years, to address
the same broken process, in a way you intend to displace. This is where the budget currently
goes. Naming it establishes:
- the buying category you belong to,
- the budget line your deal comes out of,
- credibility with a pragmatist who already understands that category.

**Product alternative** — a company harnessing similar disruptive technology, but aimed
elsewhere or built differently. Naming it establishes:
- that the new technology wave is real and you are part of it,
- your **differentiation** — specifically, that you have committed to a segment-specific whole
  product where they have not.

Moore's shorthand: *market alternatives call out the budget and thus the market category;
product alternatives call out the differentiation.*

Worked examples from the book:
- **Box** — market alternative: SharePoint (the incumbent enterprise content collaboration
  budget). Product alternative: Dropbox (the same cloud file-sharing wave, consumer-first).
  Together: "enterprise-grade, unlike Dropbox; cloud-native, unlike SharePoint."
- **Workday** — market alternative: PeopleSoft (the incumbent HR system budget). Product
  alternative: Salesforce.com (the credible SaaS wave).

Diagnostic: if you cannot find a pair of reference competitors that makes your position make
sense, that is a serious warning sign about the position itself. Moore uses Better Place — a
terrific value proposition with no credible market alternative — as the cautionary case.

Choose the market alternative deliberately: it names whose money you are taking, and that money
was already budgeted for something. Choose the product alternative deliberately: it is the
comparison your differentiation is measured against.

## The positioning process

Positioning as a verb has four components:

1. **The claim** — a claim of undisputable market leadership within a target segment, reduced to
   two sentences. Hardest of the four.
2. **The evidence** — enough proof to make disputing the claim unreasonable. Whole product
   completeness, partners, and named customers in the segment are the evidence that counts with
   pragmatists.
3. **Communications** — the right audiences, in the right sequence, with the right version of the
   message. Analysts and the trade press for the segment matter more than general tech press,
   because pragmatists communicate vertically.
4. **Feedback and adjustment** — competitors will poke holes; patch them. Positioning is a
   dynamic process, not a launch event, which means you revisit the same audiences repeatedly.
   Relationships of trust beat one-time wow.

## The claim: the elevator test

Can you explain your product in the time it takes to ride up an elevator? Moore lists what
failing this test costs you:

1. Your claim cannot travel by word of mouth — the unit of thought there is a sentence or two,
   and word of mouth is the mechanism the whole strategy depends on.
2. Your marketing communications go all over the map; each new asset picks up a different corner
   of the position, none reinforce, and the market never gets comfortable. A product with an
   uncertain position is very hard to buy.
3. Your R&D goes all over the map — engineering and product marketing pick different routes
   forward that never add up to a market advantage.
4. You cannot recruit partners and allies, because they cannot tell what you are committing to.
   What they say instead is "great technology, too bad they can't market."
5. You will not raise money from experienced investors, who use the test as a proxy for whether
   you have an investable strategy at all.

### The two-sentence formula

```
For        (target customers — beachhead segment only)
Who are dissatisfied with   (the current market alternative)
Our product is a            (product category)
That provides               (compelling reason to buy)
Unlike                      (the product alternative)
We have assembled           (key whole product features for this specific application)
```

Sentence one is the *who for / what for* position that resonates with the segment. Sentence two
foreshadows mainstream leadership by naming the differentiation against the product alternative.

Discipline in use:
- Beachhead segment only in line 1. If it says "enterprises," it is wrong.
- Line 4 must be an economic consequence, not a feature.
- **Always reinforce the second sentence.** It is the one teams drop, and it is the one that
  carries the differentiation.
- The claim is a positioning statement, not a tagline. Taglines are downstream of it.

## The competitive positioning checklist

To define the battle so you win the pragmatist's business:

1. **Focus the competition within your segment** — the combination of target customer, offer,
   and compelling reason to buy that is your primary reason for being.
2. **Create a reasonable and reasonably comprehensive competitive set.** Do not artificially
   exclude a legitimate competitor; nothing alienates a pragmatist faster.
3. **Reduce the claim to the two-sentence formula and govern every piece of communication by
   it** — reinforcing the second sentence in particular.
4. **Demonstrate the claim through the quality of the whole product and of your partners and
   allies**, so the pragmatist concludes you are — or must shortly become — the indisputable
   leader of that set.

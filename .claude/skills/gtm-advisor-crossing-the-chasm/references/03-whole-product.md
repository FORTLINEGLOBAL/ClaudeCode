# 3. Assemble the Invasion Force — The Whole Product

Source: *Crossing the Chasm*, 3rd ed., chapter 5.

## The concept

There is a gap between the marketing promise — the compelling value proposition that won the
sale — and what the shipped product can actually do. The whole product is everything that closes
that gap. (Moore credits Theodore Levitt's *The Marketing Imagination* and Bill Davidow's
*Marketing High Technology*.)

Levitt's four levels:

1. **Generic product** — what ships in the box; what the contract covers.
2. **Expected product** — what the customer thought they were buying. The minimum configuration
   of products and services needed to have *any* chance of achieving the buying objective.
3. **Augmented product** — the product fleshed out to give the *maximum* chance of achieving the
   buying objective.
4. **Potential product** — room for growth as third parties and customer-specific extensions
   accumulate. (Moore's example: the app store around a tablet.)

At the introduction of a disruptive innovation, the battle is at the generic level — the product
itself is the hero of the early market. As the market matures, generic products converge and the
battle moves to the outer rings. Crossing the chasm is exactly the point where that shift begins.

## Whole product need by psychographic

- Technology enthusiasts: need almost none; they will assemble their own.
- Visionaries: need a great deal but do not expect the vendor to supply it — they fund and
  project-manage the assembly themselves, often via systems integrators.
- **Pragmatists: evaluate and buy whole products.** They want it delivered, complete, by
  someone accountable. They will tolerate a single-vendor gap only if a credible partner
  visibly fills it.
- Conservatives: want it institutionalized, packaged, and cheap.

Consequence: after there are two or more comparable generic products in a market, further R&D
at the generic level has decreasing returns. The investment shifts to the outer rings. Deciding
where that investment goes is whole product planning.

## The simplified model — use this with clients

Two categories only:

1. **What we ship.**
2. **Everything else the customer needs to achieve their compelling reason to buy.**

Category 2 is the marketing promise. The contract does not require you to deliver it; the
customer relationship does. In B2B — where purchases are reference-driven — failing that promise
produces negative word of mouth, and sales productivity collapses.

Moore's indictment: high tech classically delivers 80–90% of a whole product to many customers
and 100% to almost none. Anything short of 100% means customers either fill the gap themselves
or feel cheated. Significantly short means the segment does not develop at all — even when the
generic product is the best in its class.

## The doughnut exercise

Draw a circle: your generic product at the center. Around it, one wedge per thing the customer
additionally needs. Typical wedges:

- Additional software / additional hardware
- System integration and interfaces to existing systems
- Installation and debugging
- Data migration
- Training and support
- Standards, procedures, and compliance artifacts
- Change management
- Financing / procurement vehicle
- Anything else required to achieve the compelling reason to buy

Then, and this is the part clients skip: **shade every wedge with an owner.** You, a named
partner, or the customer. A wedge with no owner is not a gap in the plan — it is the reason the
segment will not develop.

Two disciplines applied to the doughnut:

- **Scope it to one segment.** The whole product is defined per beachhead, per compelling reason
  to buy. A whole product for "everyone" is infinite and therefore never complete. This is the
  hidden reason the one-beachhead rule is not optional: it is what makes 100% achievable.
- **Reduce to the minimal set.** KISS. Every wedge you add is a wedge you must manage.

## Partners and allies

The point of partnering here is narrow and concrete: **fill a specific whole-product gap for a
specific beachhead segment.** Moore calls these *tactical alliances* and distinguishes them from
strategic alliances, which are about long-term market access and are mostly a distraction during
a chasm crossing.

The test for any proposed partnership: *does this close a named wedge on the doughnut for our
named beachhead within our three-month window?* If not, it is a press release, not a partnership.

Where partners come from, in practice:
- Systems integrators and VARs already serving the niche (they also solve the pragmatist's
  desire for a single point of control).
- Incumbent vendors whose product is the market alternative but who cannot deliver your piece.
- Specialist service firms in the vertical.
- In consumer/developer contexts, an ecosystem or community that supplies the outer rings for
  free — but only once there is enough traction to make contributing worthwhile.

## Moore's eight tips on whole product management

1. **Use the doughnut diagram** to define and to communicate the whole product. Shade what you
   own. The rest must be filled by partners or the customer — explicitly.
2. **Reduce to the minimal set.** It is hard enough to manage a whole product without
   unnecessary bells and whistles.
3. **Review from each participant's point of view.** Every vendor must win, and no vendor may
   take an unfair share. Inequities — especially ones favoring you — kill the effort instantly.
   Companies are already suspicious of each other; give them any encouragement and they read the
   whole scheme as a rip-off.
4. **Develop relationships slowly**, from existing instances of cooperation toward formal
   programs. Do not institutionalize cooperation before there are credible examples that
   everyone benefits — customers included. Do not recruit directly competing partners for the
   same need in the same segment; it guarantees neither commits.
5. **Large partners: bottom up. Small partners: top down.** Either way, work as close as
   possible to where decisions affecting the customer actually get made.
6. **Formal relationships are openings for communication only.** Do not expect them to drive
   cooperation. Partnerships work when specific individuals choose to trust each other.
7. **With very large partners, work the district sales office level.** Corporate staff will
   consume unlimited time and produce nothing.
8. **Expect your own company to be the hardest partner to manage.** If the deal is genuinely
   equitable, someone internal will insist on a bigger share. When you fight that, your
   customers are your most powerful allies.

## Output for the client

- The doughnut, with an owner on every wedge and dates on the unowned ones.
- The partner shortlist, each tied to a named wedge.
- The shortest credible path to 100% for the beachhead, with the gap most likely to slip called
  out by name.
